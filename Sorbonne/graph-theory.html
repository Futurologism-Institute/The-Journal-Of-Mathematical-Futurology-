FuturLang: A Computational Theory of Mathematical States, Graphs, and Prediction A FuturLang-Theoretic Reconstruction of Graphs, Principal Minors, and Eigenvalue Problems Wenitte Apiou Abstract This work develops a FuturLang-theoretic interpretation of several problems in linear algebra, probability, spectral graph theory, graph geometry, and numerical computation. The central principle of FuturLang is that a mathematical object should be understood not only by its present representation, but by the information required to distinguish, reconstruct, transform, and predict its possible states. Under this interpretation, a matrix is a state-bearing object; a graph is a relational state structure; a principal minor is an observable projection of a hidden state; an eigenvalue is a spectral invariant governing a system's modes of transformation; and an algorithm is a formally specified transition procedure between states of knowledge. The reconstruction problem therefore becomes fundamental: Given partial observations of a mathematical state, what information is sufficient to reconstruct the state, what information is fundamentally unavailable, and how efficiently can the reconstruction be performed? This perspective provides a common language for several apparently different mathematical problems. Determinantal point processes become probabilistic state-observation systems in which principal minors encode observable probabilities. Principal-minor assignment becomes a reconstruction problem: the hidden matrix must be inferred from a finite collection of observable projections. Spectral graph theory becomes a study of the possible dynamical states encoded by graph structure. The spread of a graph is interpreted as a spectral measure of the separation between extremal modes. Force-directed graph layouts become state transformations from combinatorial structure into geometric configuration. The layout problem asks which geometric state best preserves the relational structure encoded by the graph. Finally, the Lanczos method becomes a computational prediction process. Beginning with an initial vector and repeatedly applying a symmetric operator, the algorithm generates increasingly informative approximations to the extremal spectral states. Error estimates quantify the distance between the computationally observed state and the mathematical state being approximated. Futurlang therefore provides a unifying conceptual vocabulary: State→Observation→Inference→Transformation→Prediction. The mathematical results themselves remain subject to their original hypotheses and proofs. The FuturLang contribution is a change in the conceptual representation: linear algebra is interpreted as a formal language for describing, recovering, transforming, and predicting mathematical states. 1. Introduction Classical mathematical exposition frequently begins with an object. Let G=(V,E) be a graph. Let A∈Rn×n be a matrix. Let Y be a random subset. Let λ1,…,λn be eigenvalues. Futurlang begins one level earlier. It asks: What information constitutes the state of the object? and then: What transformations preserve, reveal, or destroy that information? This distinction is important because many difficult mathematical problems are not problems of constructing an object from nothing. They are problems of reconstructing an object from incomplete observations. A determinant is an observation. A principal minor is an observation. An eigenvalue is an observation. A graph drawing is an observation. A sample from a probability distribution is an observation. An approximation produced by an iterative algorithm is an observation. The underlying mathematical object may therefore be regarded as a hidden state: X. An observation procedure produces O(X). The reconstruction problem asks whether there exists a procedure R such that R(O(X))=X, possibly up to an equivalence relation. This final qualification is essential. In mathematics, two representations may be different syntactically while being equivalent structurally. Futurlang therefore distinguishes between: X=Y and X∼Y. The first expresses literal equality. The second expresses equivalence under a specified transformation. This distinction appears repeatedly throughout the mathematics considered here. 1.1 Mathematical states A Futurlang state can be represented abstractly as S=(X,I,O,T), where: ● ● ● ● X is the mathematical object; I is the available information; O is the observation operator; T is the permitted transformation system. The purpose of mathematical reasoning is then to determine relationships among these components. For example, if a matrix K generates a determinantal point process, its principal minors provide observations of K: O(K)={det⁡ (KS):S⊆[N]}. The question is not merely "what is the determinant?" The Futurlang question is: Does O(K) uniquely determine K? If not, what equivalence class does it determine? If noise is introduced, the question becomes: How much perturbation can the reconstruction tolerate? And if computation is considered: What is the minimum computational transition required to recover K? These are fundamentally questions about information and state. 1.2 Observation and hidden structure A recurring structure throughout this work is hidden mathematical state⟶observable quantities⟶reconstruction The direction of the arrow is easy. The reverse direction is difficult. A matrix produces its minors. A graph produces its spectrum. A graph produces a geometric embedding. An operator produces an iterative sequence. But recovering the source from its outputs can be substantially harder. Futurlang calls this the inverse-state problem. Given F:X→Y, the forward problem computes F(X). The inverse problem asks whether F−1(Y) can be determined. Three possibilities arise: Determinate ∣F−1(y)∣=1. The observation uniquely identifies the state. Equivalent ∣F−1(y)∣>1, but all possible preimages belong to the same relevant equivalence class. Underdetermined Distinct non-equivalent states generate the same observation. This framework becomes particularly useful for principal-minor recovery. 1.3 The four computational worlds The thesis contains four apparently independent mathematical subjects. Under Futurlang they become four forms of state computation. World I: Probabilistic states A determinantal point process describes random subsets of a finite ground set. The kernel K determines the probability structure through its principal minors. Thus: K→{det⁡ (KS)}→P(Y). The inverse problem is: P(Y)→K. World II: Spectral states A graph possesses an adjacency matrix AG. Its eigenvalues λ1≥⋯≥λn constitute a spectral representation of the graph. The spread is λ1−λn. Futurlang interprets this as a measure of separation between two extremal spectral states. The graph therefore becomes a state machine whose structure can be examined through its spectral modes. World III: Geometric states A graph can also be transformed into a geometric configuration: G→(x1,…,xn). A force-directed layout is therefore a transformation from a discrete relational state to a continuous geometric state. The optimization problem becomes: argmin⁡ xEG(x), where EG measures the discrepancy between the graph's relational structure and its geometric representation. World IV: Iterative computational states The Lanczos method generates a sequence x0,x1,x2,… whose information about the spectrum increases through successive applications of a symmetric matrix. Thus: S0→S1→S2→⋯→Sk. The algorithm does not immediately reveal the exact eigenvalue. Instead, it constructs a sequence of increasingly constrained states. The error estimate measures the remaining uncertainty. 1.4 Futurlang notation In the Futurlang interpretation, we distinguish three levels of mathematical assertion. O(x) denotes an observation. S(x) denotes a mathematical state. P(x) denotes a proved proposition concerning the state. The distinction is important. An observed numerical approximation is not identical to a theorem. A conjectured relationship is not identical to a proof. A computational experiment is not identical to an exact mathematical statement. Thus Futurlang adopts the principle: Observation≠Proof while allowing observations to generate propositions requiring proof. The Futurlang proof-demand operator may be represented as x? meaning: What formal justification establishes x? This creates a mathematical workflow: observe→hypothesize→prove→transform. 2. Determinantal Point Processes as State-Reconstruction Systems A determinantal point process is a probability distribution over subsets of a finite ground set whose probabilities are characterized by principal minors of a kernel matrix. Let [N]={1,…,N}. For a kernel K∈RN×N, the principal submatrix associated with S⊆[N] is denoted by KS. The corresponding observation is det⁡ (KS). The DPP therefore supplies a natural Futurlang example of a hidden-state system: K⟶{det⁡ (KS)}S⊆[N]⟶Y. The central computational question becomes: How much of K can be reconstructed from these observations? The original thesis studies symmetric kernels and the broader class of kernels whose corresponding off-diagonal entries have equal magnitude, and develops algorithms for learning or recovering such kernels from observations.
